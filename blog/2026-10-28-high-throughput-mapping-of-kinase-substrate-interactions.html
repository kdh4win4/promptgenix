High-throughput mapping of kinase-substrate interactions with protein language models and phospho-PCA

2026-10-28Dohoon (Roy) Kim

A pooled complementation screen paired with a protein language model assigns new substrates to 21 kinases, including the dark kinases ADCK1 and CaMKK1. The screen is the contribution. The model's claim to beat “existing models on out-of-distribution benchmarks” names no baseline, no metric and no split — and for a dark kinase, the held-out number is the only one that counts.

What it changes at the bench

Provides a method to assign upstream kinases to orphan phosphosites and characterize understudied kinases; the engineered TBK1/IKKβ biosensors and drug-repurposing candidates could inform target selection or assay development, but no clinical or preclinical validation of therapeutic hypotheses is shown yet.

Where this sits

  • Maturity: prospective wet-lab validation
  • Evidence: a preprint
  • Independently verified: no
  • Domain: target-id

Limitations

No independent replication of KINBERT benchmark claims; 'novel high-confidence substrates' rely on their own phospho-PCA assay without orthogonal biochemical validation reported; drug-repurposing candidates are computational nominations without experimental follow-up; biosensor utility demonstrated only in engineered cell lines; benchmark comparisons may favor their curated training set; generalization to full-length protein context not tested; dark kinase substrates identified for only 2 kinases.

My take

Twenty-three constructs, twenty-one kinases, two of them genuinely dark. That is a real wet-lab result and it is the part of this paper I would not argue with.

The part I would argue with is the sentence sitting next to it. KINBERT "outperforms existing models on both standard and out-of-distribution benchmarks," and the abstract names no model, no metric and no split. The comparison that matters is not obscure. Phosformer-ST (Zhou et al., Bioinformatics 2024) is the same idea — ESM2 fine-tuned on an unaligned kinase catalytic domain plus a short substrate peptide — trained on the same Johnson peptide-array corpus of 303 serine/threonine kinases. If KINBERT beats it, say by how much. If it was not run, that is the absent baseline.

The missing number is more specific than that. Phosformer-ST reports 0.995 AUC ROC on kinases it saw in training and 0.886 on kinases withheld entirely. That gap is the only figure that bears on a dark kinase, because ADCK1 and CaMKK1 are by definition the kinases with almost no literature to train on. An aggregate benchmark win says nothing about them.

"Out-of-distribution" also needs a definition here, because kinase homology makes the phrase cheap. Phosformer-ST's own paper reports that clustering its 300 kinase sequences at 70% identity leaves 187 clusters — and its held-out set was drawn by withholding roughly 10% of kinases at random, not by cluster. Split that way, a test kinase usually still has a near relative in training, so a model can interpolate within a family and look like it generalised. Any paper claiming out-of-distribution performance on kinases should say whether its split respects homology, and this one does not.

The real test for a dark kinase is loss-of-function phosphoproteomics — knock the kinase out, see which sites lose phosphate — not a complementation assay that reports proximity. For CaMKK1 that has to be genetic rather than chemical: the selective chemical probe in this family targets the paralog CAMKK2, not CaMKK1, which is exactly the situation that makes a kinase dark in the first place.

The premise behind the paper is sound, for what it is worth. Most serine/threonine phosphosites in human proteomics data still have no assigned upstream kinase, and the Dark Kinase Knowledgebase was built around 162 kinases that the literature has barely touched. The gap this paper is aiming at is real. The claim that its model closes it is the part still carrying no numbers.
