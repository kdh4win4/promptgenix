A Low-Rank Decoder Bottleneck Bounds Reliability in Foundation-Model Perturbation Prediction

2026-11-07Dohoon (Roy) Kim

An audit of STATE's decoder finds the perturbation signal enters through a near rank-one attention output — clearest for one encoder, present in all three, and that benchmark accuracy comes mostly from perturbations resembling the training set — where a ridge regression on the same embeddings does just as well.

What it changes at the bench

If you are using a foundation-model perturbation predictor to decide which CRISPRi experiments to run, this gives you a per-prediction confidence score you can compute before committing to anything — it needs only the model's own predicted response and the training responses, and the code is released. It also gives you a reason to stop reading aggregate benchmark numbers as evidence that these models can extrapolate, which is the case they are most often sold for.

What they did

The authors audit the state-transition (ST) decoder used in STATE, across three encoders — SE-600M, scGPT and Tahoe-x1 — and four cell lines (HepG2, Jurkat, RPE1, K562) under CRISPRi. Accuracy is measured as per-perturbation Pearson delta correlation (PDC). Rather than only scoring the model, they intervene inside it: replacing, projecting and ablating the outputs of individual attention layers to find where the perturbation signal actually enters.

What they found

The decoder does not beat a ridge regression. Over all held-out perturbations, each encoder+ST decoder scores within 0.04 PDC of a ridge regression reading the same input embeddings — and for two of the three encoders the ridge is ahead: 0.377 against 0.374 for SE+ST, and 0.367 against 0.337 for scGPT+ST. Only Tahoe+ST leads its ridge, 0.438 to 0.398. CPA, an older autoencoder predictor, falls in the same range. The ridge regression sees exactly what the decoder sees, so that gap is what all the attention machinery buys.

Away from training it collapses. On perturbations more than 0.5 cosine distance from every training response, the decoders fall to 0.07–0.14 PDC, against 0.07 for simply predicting the mean training response for everything. Each decoder also drops below its own ridge regression there. As the authors put it, most of the accuracy these predictors show on the benchmark comes from perturbations that resemble training perturbations.

And it degrades smoothly, not randomly. Per-perturbation PDC declines continuously with distance to the nearest training response, with the same curve shape across all three encoders and all four cell lines (Spearman ρ from −0.92 to −0.73).

The mechanism is a rank-one bottleneck. For a given perturbation, the output of the decoder's first attention layer over the control cells is close to rank one: the layer adds nearly the same vector to every control cell, scaled by a cell-specific weight. Keeping only that top component changes mean PDC by at most 0.003 for two of the three encoders. Projecting the layer's output onto just 4–32 fixed directions estimated from training activations preserves mean PDC. Replacing that first layer's output with a random direction removes one encoder's PDC entirely and more than half of another's, while the same replacement at any of the seven later layers leaves PDC essentially unchanged.

The confidence score works. Because the prediction is effectively retrieved from nearby training responses, the distance from a predicted response to its nearest training response is informative without needing the ground truth. This predicted-NN-distance reaches a mean absolute Spearman correlation of 0.81 with PDC — close to the 0.88 you get using the observed response, which you would not have in advance. A single pooled threshold works across configurations without recalibration, a Learn-then-Test procedure certifies a target accuracy in all but one configuration, and the score stays predictive for drug perturbations and at single-cell resolution.

Where the score weakens. The authors test it harder than they had to: removing each of eight gene families from training, retraining, and scoring only the held-out family. There the score stays negatively correlated with PDC in only nine of twelve configurations, and in the three RPE1 configurations the correlation is positive with an interval including zero. The paper states the consequence plainly — the score is weaker evidence of accuracy when a whole gene family is absent from training.

Limitations

Stated: the authors audited one decoder architecture with three encoders, on CRISPRi screens in four cell lines and on drug perturbations in Tahoe-100M, so the rank-one finding is established for this family rather than for perturbation models in general — and they note the bottleneck is clearest for SE+ST. The mechanism account is also incomplete by the authors' own showing — zeroing the first attention layer's output brings one encoder's PDC near zero but costs another only about a third, so part of that model's signal reaches the output another way. Two further things are worth noticing rather than being stated as limitations: PDC is a correlation across genes on delta expression, so it rewards getting the direction of a response right more than its magnitude; and the confidence score ranks predictions rather than producing an error bar, with its guarantee conditional on deployment perturbations not lying farther from training than the calibration set.

My take

I have been arguing recently that models in biology learn the shape of the data we happened to collect rather than the biology underneath it. That argument is easy to make and hard to demonstrate. This paper demonstrates it, in one system, with the model opened up.

The result I keep returning to is the ridge regression. A linear model reading the same three embeddings lands within 0.04 PDC of the full decoder on the mean across four cell lines — in fact ahead of it for two of the three encoders — and beats all three on the perturbations far from training. Everything the attention stack contributes is concentrated in the regime where the answer was nearby anyway. That is not a small caveat on an otherwise good model. It is a statement about what the model is doing, and the mechanism section confirms it: the perturbation enters through an almost rank-one attention output, and projecting onto a handful of fixed directions — between four and thirty-two — preserves accuracy. A rank-one bottleneck cannot represent very much. What it can do is index into responses the model has already seen.

I want to be precise about the claim, because it would be easy to overstate. The paper does not show that perturbation prediction is impossible, or that foundation models are useless here. It shows that this decoder, on these benchmarks, earns its score mostly through similarity to training, and that the benchmark's aggregate number conceals the split. Someone could build a model tomorrow that doesn't do this. Nobody has shown one that doesn't.

What I find genuinely useful rather than merely damning is the confidence score. The authors take their own negative result seriously enough to build something on it: if the model is retrieving, then the distance from its prediction to the nearest training response tells you how much retrieving it had to do, and that turns out to rank predictions almost as well as knowing the truth (0.81 against 0.88). That is the right response to discovering your model interpolates — not to hide it, but to expose it per prediction so the biologist can act on it. Any lab using these models to prioritise experiments should be computing something like this, and now there is code.

The part that should worry the field is the smoothness. PDC declines continuously with distance to training, with ρ between −0.92 and −0.73, the same curve for every encoder tested. That consistency means the failure is not an artefact of one model's training run. It is a property of the setup. And it means a benchmark's headline number is almost entirely determined by how its held-out split was drawn — which is a choice made by whoever built the benchmark, not a fact about biology.

My own conclusion, held at the strength the evidence supports: when someone tells me their perturbation model will let us skip experiments, the question is not what it scores. It is how far the perturbations they want to skip are from the ones already measured. If the answer is "close," the model will work and will not have saved much. If the answer is "far," it is the case the model cannot do. That is an uncomfortable shape for a technology whose entire value proposition is the second case.
