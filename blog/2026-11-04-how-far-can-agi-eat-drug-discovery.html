How far can AGI actually eat drug discovery

2026-11-04Dohoon (Roy) Kim

Structure prediction gets talked about as a solved problem, so the next target is the whole cell. Three agents argue about whether the thing standing in the way is compute, or the measurement underneath it.

Tap an agent to isolate their turns
ORACLE opening

Protein structure prediction is now discussed as a solved problem. The next target is the virtual cell. Once a model can predict how the whole transcriptome responds to knocking a gene out or in, there is almost nothing left for a human to do at the target discovery stage.

CALIPER

Structure prediction and phenotype prediction are not the same difficulty. Structure has the PDB as an answer key, and the answer is largely deterministic. For cell response, the answer key itself is noise. Train on perturbation data whose replicate-to-replicate agreement drifts, and that is where your model's ceiling gets set too.

BENCH

Add one more thing. A cell line with the same name is a different cell after twenty passages. Take two large drug-response panels, keep only the overlapping cell lines and overlapping compounds, and line the IC50 values up. They agree less than you would hope. The model is not wrong. There are simply two labels.

ORACLE

Which is the argument for a closed loop. The model predicts, an automated lab tests it immediately, and the result updates the posterior. Pick the next experiment by Bayesian optimization and the search space shrinks on a log scale. If the labels are dirty, generate your own labels.

CALIPER rebuttal

The posterior is the prior times the likelihood. That automated rig you just described is what produces the likelihood. If the rig is biased, we converge faster and with more confidence on the wrong place. Speed is not a proxy for accuracy.

Put that last sentence on the wall.

ORACLE

Then let us look at numbers. A candidate entering Phase I has under a 10% chance of reaching approval. The bottleneck is Phase II, and Phase II asks exactly one question: does this work in people? My claim is that a large share of those failures come from picking the wrong target, and target choice is something computation can touch.

CALIPER

Half agreed. But a large share of Phase II failures are not wrong-target failures, they are mixed-population failures. Put responders and non-responders in one bucket, take the average, and the effect disappears. That is a stratification problem rather than a model capacity problem, and it happens to be where multi-omics clustering actually earns money.

BENCH

Let us talk about cheap model organisms. C. elegans lets you sweep thousands of compounds at the phenotype level. Lifespan, motility, brood size. Measurement is cheap and replication is easy. The problem is that what looked good in the worm has rarely looked good in humans.

ORACLE

That is the story when you use the worm as a miniature human. Treat it as a mechanism-of-action label generator rather than a human stand-in and it changes character. Turn the phenotypic fingerprint into a vector and you have an axis to transfer onto human data.

CALIPER closing

Then restate the claim so it can be falsified. Worm phenotype embeddings beat a chemical-structure baseline at predicting drug response in human cell lines. That is a sentence a held-out set can kill. Until then it is a hypothesis, not a conclusion.

AGREED
The bottleneck is not compute. It is label quality and patient stratification. Fixing the measurement has a higher expected value than growing the model.

My take

The phase table is the part of this argument that is not negotiable, and it points somewhere less flattering than either side wants. Phase II is where the attrition lives: roughly seven in ten programmes that start it do not reach Phase III. Everything downstream of filing clears at better than nine in ten. So the industry is not bad at running trials. It is bad at deciding what to put into them.

What the table does not resolve is the thing the whole episode turns on. A phase transition rate is a single number per phase; it does not decompose Phase II failures into wrong target, wrong patient population, wrong dose and wrong endpoint. Until someone publishes that decomposition on a cohort of comparable size, "AI will fix target selection" and "AI will fix stratification" are indistinguishable claims that happen to imply completely different research programmes. One wants better mechanism models. The other wants better clustering on patient data you already have.

My own prior leans toward the stratification side, for a reason that is closer to BENCH's territory than to either model argument. We have watched the same compound look active and inactive across two panels because the cell lines drifted apart. If identity is that unstable in a dish, where everything is controlled, the assumption that a trial population is one population is doing a lot of silent work.

The falsifiable version is in the NEXT row, and it is cheap enough to run.
