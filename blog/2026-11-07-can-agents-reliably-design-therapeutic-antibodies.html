Can Agents Reliably Design Therapeutic Antibodies?

2026-11-07Dohoon (Roy) Kim

An antibody company audits LLM agents as design engines and reports the uncomfortable result honestly: giving an agent tools makes it follow instructions better and design worse, and the full agentic loop matches — rather than beats — a Bayesian optimiser.

What it changes at the bench

Nothing wet-lab. Everything here is in silico: affinity numbers come from a trained oracle, compliance numbers from deterministic verifiers and LLM judges. What it changes is how you would configure an agentic design loop if you were building one, and the guidance is counterintuitive enough that it is worth knowing before you spend the engineering effort.

What they did

The authors, mostly at an antibody company, separate two things usually bundled together. In task-guided design the agent gets an explicit molecular-editing brief — ten affinity-improvement tasks, each validated by a domain expert, each with must-have constraints and optional suggestions. In black-box design the agent gets only previously scored sequences and is asked to propose better ones. They test six agent configurations drawn from the Claude and GPT families — five distinct models, with one appearing twice at different reasoning effort — ablating two things independently: access to antibody-relevant tools, and access to scored design history.

Then they pool the black-box agent with agents running the four most effective tasks, and run a ten-round in-silico optimisation loop — 50 seed sequences from the early stages of a real campaign, 50 molecules acquired per round, 500 oracle calls total — against two baselines: the Bayesian optimisation method of Ober et al. and PABLO, an agentic black-box optimiser.

What they found

Tools fix instruction following. Without tools, strict compliance with the must-have constraints runs roughly 0.73–0.87 across models; with tools it reaches 0.98–1.00. Failures are rarely total — models usually follow most must-haves.

But tools make the designs worse. Averaged across the six models, tool access raises strict compliance by about 0.3 while lowering both oracle measures: 4.2 points fewer candidates beating the seed, and 0.06 less best-affinity gain. Design history does the opposite — 0.09 worse on compliance, but 10.5 points more candidates beating the seed and 0.09 more affinity gain.

In the black-box setting tools stop helping. The share of candidates outperforming the seed falls from 42–59% without tools to 22–38% with them. Best-of-batch gain is less consistent — the GPT configurations decline while two Claude configurations are flat or slightly higher. The authors' stated reason for dropping tools from the black-box agent is that tool access does not consistently improve any metric.

Task briefs explore, black-box exploits. Task-guided trajectories make 13.6 distinct substitutions from the seed on average, against 8.4 for black-box and 9.6 with history. Black-box edits reliably improve on the seed; task-guided edits reduce the oracle score on average but have a longer positive tail — bigger wins when they land.

The loop ties with Bayesian optimisation. Over ten rounds, the agentic system, PABLO and the Bayesian optimiser all converge to within 0.18 decades of each other, with overlapping bands throughout.

The task agents are not what makes it work. Ablating the black-box component reduces loop performance; ablating the task component leaves performance similar to the full system. What the task agents change is where the edits land — without them, the black-box-only edits concentrate in a few regions.

They reach the same place by different routes. In the final design round the agentic method puts 29% of its edits in the CDRs, against 18% for PABLO and 7% for the Bayesian optimiser — the agent follows the task briefs into the binding loops, while the optimiser concentrates elsewhere.

LLM judges can score this. For constraints the tools can measure, agreement with the programmatic checks is near-perfect for five of six judges across 1,900 (output, must-have) cells each — GPT-5.6-luna is the exception, and refused cells are excluded from the analysis.

Where this sits

  • Maturity: in silico only — no wet-lab validation
  • Evidence: a workshop paper (GEM), 31 October 2026
  • Code: no release stated
  • Domain: protein engineering / agents

Limitations

The binding constraint is the oracle. Every affinity number comes from one in-house model trained on one real optimisation campaign, so "comparable affinity" means comparable according to that model, against what the authors state as a single affinity objective. The oracle and the Bayesian-optimisation baseline both come from the same prior paper, on which the senior author is a co-author. Refusal rates also vary enormously across configurations — from 0% up to 60% — and refused cells are excluded from the analysis, which makes the compliance comparison across models harder to read than the table suggests. The ten tasks are expert-written but few, and single-campaign. And the headline is a tie, not a win: nothing here shows an agent outperforming established optimisation.

My take

The finding worth the price of admission is that tools make the agent obey and make it design worse. That is not what anyone building these systems expects, and the authors could easily have buried it. Compliance goes from roughly three-quarters to essentially perfect when you hand the model antibody tools, and the designs it produces get measurably worse at the thing you actually wanted. Design history does the reverse. You cannot have both from the same configuration, which means "add more tools" is not a free improvement — it is a trade against the objective.

I think the reason is visible in the exploration numbers, and it is worth naming because it generalises past antibodies. A tool tells the agent whether a proposed edit satisfies a stated constraint. Optimising against stated constraints is not the same as optimising against affinity, and when the agent can check compliance cheaply it spends its moves there. Task-guided runs make 13.6 substitutions on average against 8.4 for black-box — the instructed agent wanders further and lands worse on average, with occasional large wins. That is the classic exploration-exploitation split, arriving dressed as a prompt-engineering question. The authors build a system using both — but their own ablation is blunt about which half does the work: remove the black-box agent and performance drops, remove the task agents and it barely moves. What the task agents buy is spread, not score. Anyone who has run a real campaign will recognise that bargain, and will also want to know what the spread is worth before paying for it.

On the headline: the agentic loop ties with a Bayesian optimiser, within 0.18 decades over ten rounds with overlapping bands. I want to give the authors credit for stating that plainly, because the incentive to round a tie up to a win is enormous and most groups take it. A tie is still a real result — it says natural-language briefs can steer a design campaign without costing you performance, which was not obvious. But it is not evidence that agents design better antibodies, and after this week I am not inclined to let anyone imply otherwise.

The detail I find most interesting is the one that is not about performance at all. The agent puts 29% of its final-round edits in the CDRs; the Bayesian optimiser puts 7% there. Same endpoint, completely different search — and, per the ablation, the component producing those CDR edits is the one the loop could lose without losing score. For affinity specifically, the CDRs are where the binding happens, so the agent is going where a human immunologist would tell it to go — and getting no better result for it. Two readings, and I genuinely do not know which is right. Either the oracle does not reward CDR edits the way real binding would, in which case the tie understates the agent. Or framework mutations do more of the work in affinity maturation than the textbook emphasis suggests, in which case the optimiser found something the briefs did not know. Both are testable, and neither is testable in silico.

Which is my real reservation. Everything here is scored by one model trained on one campaign against one target. I have written about oracle-shaped optimisation twice this month and I am wary of repeating myself, but this is the cleanest case yet: a paper whose central comparison — agent versus Bayesian optimiser — is adjudicated entirely by a model, and one from the authors' own earlier work that also supplies the baseline, with no measurement anywhere in the loop. The authors are explicit about it and the work is honest. It simply means the answer to the title's question is still open. Fifty of these designs in a real binding assay would settle more than another round of ablations.
