Emergent Biological Capabilities in a Foundation Model for Molecular Interactions

2026-11-11Dohoon (Roy) Kim

A protein-ligand foundation model claims three zero-shot capabilities appearing sharply at its largest scale, plus 131 confirmed inhibitors from prospective wet-lab work on nine targets. The experiments are real and the caveats are stated — including that each scale is a single training run and the weights are not released.

What it changes at the bench

If the wet-lab numbers hold up under replication, this is a screening tool that ranks de novo molecules against a target from sequence alone, at about 2,500 pairs per second on one H100, or 42,500 with cached representations — fast enough to change what you bother to ask. The catch for anyone wanting to use it is in the availability section: weights and training data are not public, and access is case by case.

What they did

Bonbon is a transformer trained by self-supervision on approximately 3.2 trillion tokens of paired protein-ligand sequences — proprietary screening data enriched with synthetic interaction data generated to match its distributional properties, with that generation method deferred to a separate report — at a 1:2,000 parameter-to-token ratio — two orders of magnitude past Chinchilla-optimal, chosen because paired interaction data is sparse. No structural coordinates, no task labels.

The paper then asks what falls out of the frozen representations at three resolutions: mechanism of action, binding-site location (including orthosteric versus allosteric), and covalent versus non-covalent engagement. A controlled scaling experiment runs six sizes — 42M, 315M, 800M, 1B, 1.3B, 1.6B — under identical conditions except parameter count. Finally, molecules generated de novo under synthetic-accessibility constraints were ranked against nine disease targets, and up to 24 compounds per target from the top of each ranking were synthesised by a CRO and assayed.

What they found

Three capabilities, none supervised. A 160-feature signature over 107,027 interactions reaches 81.1% macro precision across six mechanism classes; separately, within-receptor agonist/antagonist discrimination on 28,126 interactions across 222 GPCRs — a design that holds the receptor constant to remove protein identity as a shortcut — reaches 85.5% balanced accuracy. Attention weights track ligand proximity: in-distribution, residues within 5 Å receive 1.74× the per-protein mean and residues beyond 25 Å 0.58×, with 1.71× and 0.52× on held-out proteins, and roughly 25% of heads self-organise for spatial localisation. Residue-level binding-site F1 runs 0.274–0.461 across protein families in distribution and 0.252–0.409 out of it, so it holds up OOD. Covalent-versus-non-covalent AUROC reaches 0.838.

A jump at the last scale. Covalent AUROC across the six scales runs 0.645, 0.678, 0.691, 0.675, 0.695, then 0.838. Pretraining loss meanwhile falls smoothly from 6.94 to 1.44. The per-tenfold-compute slopes for the three capabilities are 0.012, 0.008 and 0.005, against 0.682, 0.780 and 0.437 for the single 1.3B-to-1.6B step. The authors' point is that loss-based scaling gives no warning that a capability is about to appear.

They take the "mirage" objection seriously. Schaeffer and colleagues argued apparent emergence is often an artefact of threshold-imposing metrics. Here each capability is measured on a continuous metric and on a non-overlapping protein set.

Prospective wet lab on nine targets. Confirmed inhibitors: TYK2 23 (146 nM–40 µM), PRMT5 16 (186 nM–143 µM), JAK1 21 (3 nM–28 µM), CDK1 22 of 22 tested (114 nM–23 µM), HDAC6 15 (115 nM–22 µM, 12 without a hydroxamic acid), MNK2 19 (39 nM–38 µM, 18 novel), MMP-9 3 (480 nM–1.3 µM, none with a strong zinc-chelating warhead), and on two targets absent from training entirely, ELANE 2 and VEGFR2 10 (38 nM–82 µM). That is 131 compounds, reported as a 61.5% dose-response hit rate. All tested compounds sit below 0.60 Tanimoto to anything in training.

Where this sits

  • Maturity: prospective wet-lab validation
  • Evidence: a preprint, 10 November 2026, single author, company-affiliated
  • Weights and training data: not released; access case by case, stated as a safety mitigation. Code is not mentioned either way
  • Domain: protein-ligand modelling / drug discovery

Limitations

The authors state the one that bears most on the headline: "Each scale is a single training run. Training-seed variability was not measured." A jump at the largest of six single runs cannot be separated from run-to-run variation without replicates, whatever the metric's continuity.

A confirmed inhibitor is defined, but loosely: a compound active in the single-dose primary screen whose dose-response series then returns a determinable IC50 from a four-parameter logistic fit. There is no potency bar, and the numeric progression criterion from the 10 µM primary screen is never given. The dilution series tops out at 100 µM, yet PRMT5's reported range reaches 143 µM, so that value sits above the highest concentration tested. A hit rate is only interpretable against a potency bar, and there isn't one. Binding-site F1 around 0.25–0.46 is a modest absolute number, notable mainly because it is zero-shot from sequence. The authors also list their own scope limits: capabilities resolve coarse distinctions, no 3D coordinate extraction, nothing on residence time or dissociation kinetics. And because neither weights nor training data are released — and part of the training corpus is synthetic by a method not yet described — none of the model-side results is independently checkable right now.

My take

I have spent most of this month writing about papers whose measurements could not carry their claims, so let me start with what this one does that those did not: it went to the bench. Nine targets, compounds synthesised by a CRO, ten-point dose-response in duplicate with reference inhibitors on every plate, two targets absent from training entirely. Whatever else is true, somebody made the molecules and measured them. That is rarer than it should be and it deserves saying first.

Now the part I would want answered before I believed the headline. A 61.5% hit rate — 131 confirmed out of 213 synthesised — is a striking number, and a hit rate means nothing without a potency bar. There isn't one. The paper does define a confirmed inhibitor: active in the 10 µM primary screen, then a determinable IC50 from a four-parameter fit. That is a threshold on curve-fittability, not on potency. PRMT5's range reaches 143 µM, above the 100 µM top of the dilution series, so that value is extrapolated from a partial curve. I am not saying the compounds are inactive. I am saying "confirmed inhibitor" is carrying an enormous amount of weight in that percentage. Set the bar at 10 µM and it would be a different number, and the per-target tables have what you'd need to recompute it. The per-target detail is all there in the appendix, which is to the author's credit, and it is also what makes the omission conspicuous.

On emergence I want to be fairer than my instinct. The usual objection — Schaeffer and colleagues' argument that emergence is a metric artefact — is anticipated here properly, with continuous metrics and non-overlapping protein sets, which is more care than most papers making this claim take. The covalent AUROC series is a genuinely striking shape: flat from 42M through 1.3B, then 0.838. But the author also writes, plainly, that each scale is a single training run and seed variability was not measured. That sentence is the whole ballgame. A single jump at the largest of six single runs is exactly what run-to-run variance looks like when you have one run each, and no metric design fixes a missing replicate. I would want three seeds at 1.3B and three at 1.6B. That is a cheap experiment relative to what was already spent, and until it exists "emergent" is a description of a plot rather than a finding about the model.

The result I find most interesting is the one the abstract undersells. On HDAC6, twelve of fifteen confirmed inhibitors lack a hydroxamic acid. Every FDA-approved HDAC inhibitor has one, and its toxicity has bottlenecked that field for two decades. On MMP-9, none of the three carries a strong zinc-chelating warhead. Whatever you think of the hit-rate framing, a ranking model that walks away from the field's one universal pharmacophore and still finds activity is doing something more interesting than recall. That is the claim I would build the next paper around, and it needs far fewer caveats than emergence does.

My reservation of principle: the weights and training data are not released, framed as a safety mitigation. I take dual-use concerns in this area seriously and I am not going to pretend the argument is empty. But it does mean that a single-author, non-peer-reviewed preprint is making unusually large claims that nobody outside the company can check — not the 3.2 trillion tokens, part of which is synthetic data generated by a method deferred to a future report, not the scaling curves, not the ranking that produced the compounds. The wet-lab results are at least in principle reproducible by anyone willing to synthesise the structures, if those are disclosed. I would weight this the way I weight any strong industrial result with a closed model behind it: interesting, plausible, and provisional until someone else gets the same answer.
