The Literature Is Not a Sample of Biology

2026-11-06Dohoon (Roy) Kim

Every model we train in biology learns from the published record. That record tracks what was easy to measure in 1995 far more closely than it tracks what matters in disease — and no amount of scaling fixes a biased sampling process.

I spend my days training and evaluating models on biological data, and I have come to think the field's central problem is not in the models at all. It is in the corpus. We talk about data as if it were a window onto biology. It is not. It is a record of what a particular population of humans, working under a particular incentive structure, found tractable enough to measure and interesting enough to publish. Those are not the same thing as biology, and the gap between them is large, directional, and measurable.

The skew is measurable, not a feeling

The best single piece of evidence I know is Stoeger and colleagues' 2018 analysis in PLoS Biology. They asked a simple question — why do some human genes get studied and others not — and answered it quantitatively.

Their headline finding is that most research on human genes concentrates on approximately 2,000 of the roughly 19,000 genes in the human genome. By their fractional-counting measure, 27% of human genes have never been the subject of a full publication. Not under-studied. Never studied.

Then they asked what predicts a gene's share of attention. Using gradient boosting over 430 features, they predicted publication counts with a Spearman correlation of 0.64, and fifteen features did most of the work: RNA and protein abundance, sensitivity to mutation, protein charge and hydrophobicity, transcript length, the presence of a signal sequence. Read that list again. Every item on it is a property that determined whether you could get a clean blot, raise an antibody, or clone a construct with 1990s reagents. The authors say so directly: research has been guided by "a handful of generic chemical and biological characteristics" that "facilitated experimentation during the 1980s and 1990s, rather than the physiological importance of individual genes or their relevance to human disease."

The history compounds it. Genes first reported by 1991 make up 16% of all genes, and they account for 49% of the literature of 2015. Add a gene's year of first report to the model and prediction rises to 0.75. Add the publication counts of its model-organism homologues and it reaches 0.87. Meanwhile, disease associations on their own predicted NIH funding at only 0.43.

So: how much a gene gets studied is better predicted by when it was first cloned and how well it behaves in a tube than by whether it has anything to do with human disease. The authors estimate that at current rates it would take at least five decades before all genes are adequately characterised.

The same shape shows up wherever I look. In pharmacology, 667 human proteins are the targets of approved drugs — about 3.5% of roughly 20,000 human proteins. In the kinome, the single most intensively drugged protein family in oncology, the Dark Kinase Knowledgebase catalogues 162 understudied kinases out of a family numbering "well over 500," with nearly a third of the family having largely unknown function. We have spent thirty years and an enormous amount of money on kinases, and a third of them are still dark.

And a large share of what is in the record does not hold

The coverage problem would be survivable if the covered part were solid. It is not reliably solid.

Amgen scientists took 53 landmark preclinical cancer papers and were able to confirm the findings in 6 of them — 11%. Bayer looked at 67 in-house target-validation projects and found that only about 20–25% had published data completely in line with what they saw internally.

The most instructive one is the Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology, because it documents the mechanism rather than just the rate. The project set out to replicate 193 experiments from 53 papers. It completed 50 experiments from 23 papers. For the original positive results, the median replication effect size was 85% smaller than the median original effect size. And the detail I keep returning to, reported in the project's companion paper on the challenges of the attempt: none of the 193 experiments were described in sufficient detail in the original paper to design a replication without contacting the original authors. For 32% of the experiments, the original authors were not at all helpful or did not respond.

That last finding is not a story about misconduct. It is a story about tacit knowledge. A methods section is a summary written for reviewers, not an executable protocol. The actual experiment lives in a postdoc's hands, in a particular lot of serum, in a passage number nobody recorded, in the ten minutes somebody left a plate on the bench. Most of the information required to reproduce a biological result was never written down anywhere, and the people who held it have moved on.

What a model trained on this corpus actually learns

Put the two problems together and consider what a model fits.

It does not fit biology. It fits the product of biology and tractability — the joint distribution of what is true and what was convenient to measure. These two factors are entangled in every training example, and you cannot disentangle them after the fact, because the confound is the sampling process itself. The genes with the most data are the genes that were abundant, soluble, well-behaved, and cloned early. A model will be confident about exactly those, and its confidence will be well-calibrated on a test set drawn from the same distribution, which is to say on more of the same genes.

This is why I am sceptical of the argument that scale alone will carry us. More parameters over the same corpus gives you a sharper estimate of the same biased quantity. More text gives you more of the 2,000 genes. Neither touches the 27%.

I want to be fair about where this argument does not apply. Protein structure prediction worked, and the PDB is also a biased sample — crystallisable, soluble, stable, and heavily redundant in favour of a few folds. The difference is that structure has a physical objective and a shared ground truth. A coordinate file can be checked against the physics. There is no comparable anchor for "what does perturbing this gene do in a human cell," because the ground truth is an assay someone chose, in a cell line that has drifted, on a day with its own batch effect. The AlphaFold precedent is real, and it does not transfer to phenotype as cleanly as the field's optimism assumes.

The lever is the experiment, not the model

If the corpus is the binding constraint, then the only intervention that changes the trajectory is generating a different corpus — not a bigger draw from the same distribution.

What that would require is not mysterious. Coverage chosen by design rather than by publishability. Negative results retained rather than discarded. Batch structure recorded as first-class metadata rather than reconstructed afterwards by someone like me with a linear model and a prayer. Protocols that are executable artefacts rather than prose. Replication built into the generating process instead of attempted years later by strangers who have to email the original authors.

Every one of those is a scheduling and bookkeeping problem before it is a scientific one. And every one of them is something a machine is better suited to than a person — not because the machine is cleverer, but because it is indifferent. An automated system has no reason to prefer the gene with 4,000 papers over the one with none. It has no grant renewal, no thesis committee, no need for the result to be interesting. Indifference is the feature.

My take

I will state this more strongly than any of the papers above do, because it is my own view and I have been living inside it for twelve years.

The rate-limiting step in computational biology has not been algorithms for some time. It is that our training data was collected by people who were, entirely rationally, optimising for publishability. Every incentive in academic biology pushes toward the tractable, the already-popular, and the positive. Those three filters are applied before a single row of data reaches me, and they are applied by thousands of independent labs in roughly the same direction, which is what makes the bias systematic rather than noisy. I cannot correct for it downstream. Nobody can. You cannot debias a sample by looking harder at the sample.

This is why I have changed my mind about where effort should go. I used to think the interesting work was better architectures and better benchmarks. I now think benchmarks built on this corpus mostly measure how well a model has learned the corpus's own blind spots, and that the genuinely high-leverage work is in how the data gets made. If I could redirect one dollar in computational biology, it would not go to a larger model. It would go to instrumented, automated, uniformly-sampled experimentation that keeps its failures.

The part of this I hold most firmly: the 27% of genes with no publications are not unimportant. There is no mechanism by which they could be. They are the genes that were hard to work with in 1995. Somewhere in that set is a substantial fraction of what we still do not understand about immunology and pharmacology — and we will not find it by reading more of what we have already written down.

I will say what would change my mind. If a model trained purely on the existing literature and public datasets makes a confident, prospective, out-of-distribution prediction about an understudied gene — one with essentially no prior publications — and that prediction holds up in an independent lab, then the corpus contains more transferable structure than I am crediting it with, and I am wrong about the size of the problem. I have not seen that yet. I would genuinely like to.
