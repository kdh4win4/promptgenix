When Gene Summaries Reveal the Answers: Gene Ontology Circularity in Gene Embedding Benchmarks

2026-11-11Dohoon (Roy) Kim

NCBI gene summaries are partly machine-generated from Gene Ontology annotations — the same annotations used to score gene embeddings. They cover 32% of human protein-coding genes, and 58% of the ones with only a handful of publications: exactly the genes where the benchmark is supposed to be hard.

What it changes at the bench

If you have ever evaluated a gene-embedding model by how well it recovers GO terms, this tells you that part of the score came from the model reading those terms in prose. If you are choosing between encoders, it also says the ranking you get depends on which text each encoder was fed — so a leaderboard built on mixed-provenance text is comparing corpora as much as models.

What they did

The Alliance of Genome Resources generates gene descriptions by converting GO annotations into sentences. NCBI Gene displays those descriptions, and GenePT feeds them to an encoder. So the input text can contain the very labels the evaluation scores against.

The authors quantify it: they embed the same corpus with and without the generated summaries, refit a function-prediction probe on each version, and compare per-gene AUROC — stratifying by how many publications a gene has, since that is what the coverage tracks. They add replacement controls, an experimental-evidence filter, six encoders, and two further species, then package the whole thing as a benchmark, GeneProv, that compares encoders on matched text.

What they found

The exposure is wide, and worst where it matters. Generated summaries covered 6,172 of 19,256 human protein-coding genes (32.1%) — including 58% of genes with one to ten linked publications. The under-studied genes, the ones a useful embedding would actually help with, are the ones most likely to carry a sentence spelling out their annotations.

Removing the text costs real score. Among genes with one to ten publications, removal dropped per-gene AUROC by 0.078 with a linear probe (95% bootstrap 0.072–0.084) and 0.033 with an MLP, stable across three further fold seeds.

It is not just "having a description." Matched on exposure and GO-term count, removing generated summaries cost 0.141 while removing RefSeq summaries cost 0.054 — a difference of 0.087. And among genes with one to ten publications, replacing each generated summary with one written by Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct cost 0.084 relative to the original text, landing 0.007 below using the gene symbol alone.

Filtering to experimental evidence does not fix it. Under the experimental-evidence filter, the removal contrast among 1–10-publication genes was still 0.098 with the linear probe. The Alliance gives experimental evidence priority when choosing which terms to narrate, so the strictest filter retains the sentences most likely to name the answer.

It is not a large-language-model artifact. A plain TF-IDF encoder benefits too, by 0.043.

Other species are worse. Generated-summary coverage at one to ten publications ran 90–98% across six species, against 57% in human. In mouse, removal cost 0.165 against MGI annotations. In yeast, where every available NCBI summary is generated, NCBI text scored 0.954 against 0.902 for descriptions written by SGD curators, 0.858 for ESM2-650M and 0.731 for symbols alone.

Replacing the source recovers much of it — which is its own problem. Swapping in UniProt function descriptions restores much of the lost performance despite little verbatim overlap. UniProt also curates many of the evaluation labels.

Where this sits

  • Maturity: benchmark and audit, in silico
  • Evidence: a preprint, 8 November 2026
  • Artifact: GeneProv benchmark released
  • Domain: gene embeddings / evaluation

Limitations

The authors are unusually direct about a flaw in their own numbers: the estimates use a historical GO scorer that mishandled ties, and the original predictions are no longer available to rescore. They set aside the nearest-neighbour probe entirely for this reason, since its label-vector predictions produce many ties and are therefore most sensitive to the error. The headline contrasts still run through that scorer, and the paper states plainly that without the original predictions the effect on the GO contrasts and composite ranks is unknown — so the direction and the stratification are solid while the exact magnitudes are provisional. Prediction was tested in two of the six organisms surveyed. And the Qwen replacement is described as a post hoc control rather than a pre-planned arm.

My take

I wrote an essay last week arguing that the published record is a biased sample of biology and that models trained on it inherit the bias. This paper is the tighter, nastier version of that point: in gene-embedding benchmarks, the record does not merely bias the model, it leaks the answer key.

The number that matters is 58%. That is the share of genes with one to ten publications carrying an auto-generated summary — a sentence assembled out of the GO annotations the benchmark then asks the model to recover. Think about where that sits. Genes with thousands of papers do not need an embedding to tell you what they do. The entire value proposition of a gene-embedding model is the thin end of the distribution, the genes nobody has characterised. And that is precisely the stratum where the input text is most likely to contain the label.

I had assumed the standard defences covered this. They do not. Filtering to experimental evidence makes it slightly worse rather than better, because the Alliance prioritises experimental evidence when deciding which terms to narrate — the strictest filter keeps the most informative sentences. And the effect is not an artefact of some language model doing clever inference: TF-IDF picks up 0.043 of it. That is string-matching. There is no interpretive step to blame.

The control I find most damning is the Qwen replacement. Swap each generated summary for one written by a 7B model and you lose 0.084 — ending up 0.007 below using the bare gene symbol. A paraphrase of the same biology is worth less than nothing relative to the original. That is a very specific signature. It says the value was never "a description of this gene exists"; it was the particular lexical form of the annotation.

The yeast result is the cleanest demonstration in the paper and I would use it in a lecture. Every available NCBI yeast summary is machine-generated. NCBI text scores 0.954. Descriptions written by SGD curators — real experts, describing the same genes — score 0.902. A protein language model gets 0.858, the gene symbol alone 0.731. The generated text beats the human experts, and it beats them because it is downstream of the scoring key rather than upstream of the biology.

Two things I would hold back on. The authors flag that their estimates came through a GO scorer that mishandled ties and that they cannot rescore the original predictions; they set aside one probe entirely over it. I would treat the direction and the stratification as solid and the second decimal place as provisional. And the UniProt result is genuinely unresolved rather than damning — swapping in UniProt text recovers much of the performance with little verbatim overlap, which could mean UniProt prose carries real functional signal, or could mean the circularity runs one level deeper, since UniProt also curates many of the labels. The paper does not claim to settle it and neither will I.

What I take away is operational. If you are benchmarking gene representations, the question is no longer "what does it score" but "what text did it read, and who wrote that text." GeneProv exists now to make that comparison on matched inputs, and I do not think a leaderboard that ignores provenance is reporting a property of the models at all.
