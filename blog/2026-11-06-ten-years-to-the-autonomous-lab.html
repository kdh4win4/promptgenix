Ten Years to the Autonomous Lab, and What Self-Driving Actually Proves

2026-11-06Dohoon (Roy) Kim

The self-driving comparison is the right analogy for automated science, but not for the reason it usually gets used. The gain did not come from a better driver. It came from a fleet that keeps every mile — and biology has no such fleet.

I think experiments will be run predominantly by automated systems within about ten years, and I think the reason has very little to do with robots being more precise than postdocs. It has to do with variance, bookkeeping, and the fact that a machine will keep data a human throws away. The self-driving comparison is the one I reach for, and it is worth being careful about what that comparison actually establishes.

What the self-driving numbers say

Waymo's engineers published a comparison of their rider-only crash rates against human benchmarks over 56.7 million driverless miles through the end of January 2025. Against the human benchmark they report 79% fewer any-injury-reported crashes (95% CI 71–85%), 81% fewer crashes with airbag deployment (69–90%), 92% fewer pedestrian injury crashes (66–99%), and 82% fewer cyclist injury crashes (41–97%).

The caveats are real and the authors state them. All of them are employed by Waymo. No corrections were made for multiple comparisons. The serious-injury result — 85% fewer, with a confidence interval running from 39% to 99% — rests on two autonomous-system crashes, and the authors call it "an indication of an effect" rather than a finding. The significance of some results is sensitive to the choice of benchmark, mileage is self-reported, and crashes are counted regardless of fault.

Even discounting heavily for all of that, the direction and magnitude are not subtle. But the mechanism is the part people skip. The improvement is not that the system is a better driver than a good human on a given mile. It is that no human driver has ever accumulated 56.7 million miles of instrumented experience, and no human driver's near-miss has ever updated every other driver's behaviour. The unit that got better is the fleet, not the driver. Every mile is logged, every intervention is reviewed, every failure is retained and fed back. A human driver's hundred thousand close calls die with the driver.

That is the property I want in a laboratory, and it is the one we conspicuously lack.

What autonomous labs actually deliver today

The clearest existing demonstration is the A-Lab at Berkeley. Over 17 days of continuous operation it worked through a set of 57 targets proposed by human researchers and realised 36 of them, a 63% success rate, with what the paper calls minimal human intervention. Manually regrinding the products of two further targets raised the rate to 67%. Over roughly a year and a half of operation the hardware exception rate averaged about 3.9%.

So the execution worked. Seventeen days, essentially unattended, at a 3.9% fault rate. That is a real result and I do not want it lost in what follows.

The instructive part is what happened next. Josh Leeman, Leslie Schoop, Robert Palgrave and colleagues re-analysed the reported discoveries and published their analysis in PRX Energy. Their conclusion was blunt: "These errors unfortunately lead to the conclusion that no new materials have been discovered in that work." The central technical criticism was that the pipeline could not handle compositional disorder — by Palgrave's account, two thirds of the predictions were ordered versions of compounds already known to be disordered, so the synthesis produced known disordered phases while the system logged them as new ordered ones. He described the automated Rietveld refinement of the diffraction data as "very bad, very beginner, completely novice human level."

Gerbrand Ceder, who led the A-Lab work, replied first in a LinkedIn post in December 2023, arguing that the syntheses held up while conceding the analytical point directly: "We have no doubt that a human can perform a higher-quality refinement on these samples."

The formal resolution arrived in January 2026, when the authors published a correction in Nature. They re-analysed the diffraction patterns by hand and reported that the platform "came to the correct conclusion in 36 of its 40 reported successes, with 4 compounds being inconclusive." They removed one compound, Zn2Cr3FeO8, which had been "mistakenly included in the training data." And on novelty they conceded the framing entirely: the original claims "were subject to misinterpretation," and the intention "was to indicate that the materials were new to the prediction platform, not necessarily new to science."

I think that episode contains the whole argument of this essay. The robot's weak link was not pipetting, heating or scheduling — those parts worked, continuously, for 17 days. The weak link was deciding what the measurement meant, and then knowing whether the answer was already sitting in its own training data. Automation moved the bottleneck from execution to interpretation and ran straight into it. It then took more than two years and a journal correction for humans to settle what the machine had actually found.

The hypothesis layer is further ahead than the execution layer

This asymmetry shows up again in the systems people point to when they say the big labs are automating science.

Google's AI co-scientist is a multi-agent system built on Gemini 2.0. It is software. It generates hypotheses, research overviews and proposed protocols from a natural-language goal; it does not hold a pipette. It was assessed on 15 research goals curated by seven domain experts, with a smaller set of 11 used for expert novelty and impact judgements. Its reported validations are genuine and worth taking seriously: proposed drug-repurposing candidates for acute myeloid leukaemia that inhibited tumour viability at clinically relevant concentrations in multiple AML cell lines; epigenetic targets with anti-fibrotic activity in human hepatic organoids, with p-values below 0.01; and independent recapitulation of an unpublished finding about cf-PICIs interacting with diverse phage tails to expand host range.

Every one of those wet-lab experiments was run by humans — collaborators at Stanford, and at the Fleming Initiative and Imperial College London. Google's own write-up notes that the system's Elo self-rating "is not based on an independent ground truth."

I am not saying that to diminish the work. I am saying it because it inverts the assumption most people hold. We are not short of hypotheses, and we are not short of compute to generate them. We are short of the capacity to test them, and that capacity is still almost entirely human, manual, and slow. The generative layer has run ahead of the experimental layer, and the gap is widening.

Where the analogy holds, and where it breaks

It holds in two places. First, removing the human removes the variance — the batch effects, the passage-number drift, the Friday-afternoon plate, the lot-to-lot antibody difference that nobody wrote down. Second, and more importantly, automation makes the fleet possible: a system that logs every run, including the failures, and updates on all of them.

It breaks in two places, and I think they are the places that actually determine the ten-year question.

A car has one objective and a shared physics. Do not hit anything, obey the rules, get there. Every Waymo in every city is solving the same problem under the same constraints, which is why pooled experience transfers. A biological experiment has no such shared objective. The hard part is not executing the assay; it is deciding whether the readout means what you hoped it meant — which is exactly where the A-Lab ran aground, in a domain with far cleaner ground truth than cell biology.

And there is no telemetry commons in biology. Waymo keeps every near-miss because near-misses are the most valuable data it has. A lab discards the failed western, the construct that would not express, the cell line that stopped responding. Those failures are the negative space that would tell a model where the boundaries are, and we systematically delete them. Until failures are retained, there is no fleet — only a very large number of individual drivers who each learn nothing from the others. This is the same problem as the publication bias in the literature, arriving from the other direction.

What the ten-year claim actually requires

So I will put conditions on my own prediction rather than leave it as a slogan.

For synthesis-dominated fields — inorganic materials, a good deal of synthetic chemistry, enzyme engineering with a clean selectable readout — I think predominantly automated experimentation inside ten years is close to a straight-line extrapolation. The objective is well-defined, the characterisation is instrumental, and A-Lab already ran for 17 days.

For biology, the decade turns on closing the characterisation gap, not the automation gap. Liquid handling is a solved problem that money can buy. What is not solved is automated interpretation of a noisy, context-dependent readout from a living system whose state nobody fully specified. I would want three things to be true, and I would treat them as the markers worth watching: assays whose validity is machine-checkable rather than argued in a discussion section; negative results retained by default, at fleet scale, because the system's own learning depends on them; and target selection driven by coverage rather than by what is already well-represented in the literature.

Get those three and ten years looks conservative. Miss them and we will have built something that can run a bad experiment faster than anyone has ever run one before.

My take

My position, stated plainly: I think human-run experiments will look, within my working lifetime, roughly the way human-driven cars look to me now — obviously the thing we had to do first, and obviously not the thing we should still be doing at scale.

I hold that view for a specific reason, and it is not that scientists are careless. It is that a person cannot do the bookkeeping. I have spent years as the person downstream, trying to recover batch structure from experiments where it was never recorded, trying to reconcile two labs' versions of the same assay, trying to decide whether an effect is biology or the week it was run in. That work is not hard because the biologists were sloppy. It is hard because the information was never captured, and a human running an experiment at the bench has no realistic way to capture it. A machine captures it for free. That is the whole argument, and it is a boring argument, which is why I believe it.

Where I would push back on my own enthusiasm — and the A-Lab exchange above is what pushed me — is the assumption that automating execution gets you automated science. It does not. It gets you a faster path to the real bottleneck, which is interpretation. The Leeman–Schoop–Palgrave critique is, read generously, the best news in this field: a robot ran for 17 days without stopping, and the thing that failed was the analysis — a point the A-Lab authors themselves accepted in print two years later. That is a tractable problem. It is a much more tractable problem than building the robot was.

The part I feel most strongly about is why any of this matters. In immunology and in pharmacology, the honest position is that we do not know most of it. Six hundred and sixty-seven human proteins are targets of approved drugs, out of around twenty thousand. Nearly a third of the kinome is still dark after three decades of intensive kinase drug discovery. These are not gaps that will close at the current rate; the arithmetic says fifty years at best for gene coverage alone. I do not think patients have fifty years, and I do not think the answer is asking scientists to work harder. The answer is raising the throughput of trustworthy experimentation by an order of magnitude or more, and the only way I can see to do that is to take the human out of the execution loop and put them where they are irreplaceable — choosing what is worth asking, and judging whether the answer is real.

Here is my falsifiable version. By 2036, in at least one therapeutic area, I expect the majority of target-validation experiments supporting a programme entering the clinic to have been executed by automated systems with no human at the bench, and I expect that programme's attrition rate to be measurably better than the historical baseline. If the automation arrives but attrition does not improve, then I was wrong about the cause: the variance was never the binding constraint, and we will have bought speed without buying truth. That is the outcome I would most want to know about early.
