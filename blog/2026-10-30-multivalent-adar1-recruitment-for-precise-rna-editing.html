Multivalent ADAR1 recruitment for precise RNA editing

2026-10-30 · Dohoon (Roy) Kim

Multivalent recruitment of endogenous ADAR1 p150 through its Za domain reports up to 100% A-to-I editing in vitro and 84% in vivo. The efficiency number is the one being advertised; the specificity number is the one that would settle it.

What it changes at the bench

Provides a potentially more efficient tool for transcript-level correction of disease-associated mutations without requiring exogenous ADAR delivery; may expand options for therapeutic RNA editing if the in vivo results hold in disease models.

Where this sits

  • Maturity: prospective wet-lab validation
  • Evidence: a peer-reviewed paper
  • Independently verified: no
  • Domain: other

Limitations

100% in vitro and 84% in vivo are headline claims without detail on n, cell types, or delivery method in this abstract. Bystander editing rates not quantified in abstract. No disease model efficacy shown here. Durability, immunogenicity, and off-target transcriptome-wide effects unaddressed. Single-study validation only; no independent replication.

My take

Eighty-four percent in vivo is a large number for this field, and the first thing I would want is the denominator.

Editing percentages in ADAR-recruiting work are reported in ways that are not interchangeable. The fraction of transcripts edited in cells that actually received the guide is a different quantity from the fraction edited across a whole tissue, and both are different from what you would measure in an organ where delivery is uneven. Liver flatters every delivery technology. Until the paper says which tissue, which transcript, how editing was measured and what the delivery efficiency was, 84% is a headline rather than a result.

The second thing is harder to shrug off. The abstract opens by naming bystander editing as the problem with existing systems, and closes with an efficiency figure and no specificity figure. If reduced bystander editing is the contribution, the bystander rate is the number that belongs next to the 84%. Its absence from the summary does not mean it is absent from the paper, but it does tell you which result the authors chose to lead with.

Third, recruiting p150 specifically through its Za domain is not a neutral choice. That isoform's ordinary job includes keeping MDA5 from treating endogenous double-stranded RNA as an infection. A system designed to grab it multivalently is, by construction, competing with that function, and the interferon consequences of getting this wrong in ADAR1 biology are well documented. Transcriptome-wide RNA-seq, not target-site amplicon counts, is what would answer it.

What I would not dismiss: recruiting endogenous ADAR means no exogenous enzyme to deliver or raise antibodies against, and that structural advantage is why this class is worth improving at all.

Worth remembering where the clinical bar actually sits. The first human RNA-editing readout, in alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, was reported as protein output and durability — wild-type M-AAT around 6.9 micromolar, more than 60% of total AAT, after a single dose — not as an editing percentage. Edited RNA turns over. A single high timepoint in a mouse says little about what repeat dosing looks like.
